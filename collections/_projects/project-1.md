Floods, storms, and earthquakes flush sediment downslope in sediment gravity flows, and these flows settle into distinct sediment layers on the seabed. Over time, these layers stack up, and geologists date them to infer the timing and frequency of prior disturbance. This is prominent on the Cascadia Margin, where turbidites in canyon channels are dated and attributed to subduction zone earthquakes. There is no comprehensive accounting for the false positives or negatives that may linger in this sediment record.

I take a slightly different approach to the turbidite dataset: what if the composition of a turbidite can provide an indication of where it originated? Different types of flows originate from different locations along the continental margin, and through this lens, composition can give us overlooked clues into the process that triggered that flow.

By analyzing the most recently deposited event beds sediment cores for age, composition, and density, we can differentiate between flood-deposited layers and earthquake-triggered debris flows. I study this within Astoria Canyon, which is a special environment to test these methods, as it contains a ~300 year record exceptionally preserved in suboxic waters preventing animals from burrowing into these layers and destroying them. Specifically, we date layers to the 1948 and 1894 floods of the Columbia River, and a major debris flow layer to the 1700 earthquake. 

In the upper canyon, flood turbidites are typically thin and laminated with terrestrial material, while earthquake-driven deposits are meter-scale and far more energetic, carrying individual clasts up to 50 cm in diameter. These contrasts allow scientists to separate the origins and triggers of sediment layers which otherwise may be conflated. Understanding these differences helps clarify which events actually triggered the sediment movement and how they shape the canyon over time.

The study confirms that canyon heads continue to initiate sediment gravity flows during sea level highstand--a key "false positive" to be wary of when interpreting earthquake records. Also, earthquake-triggered flows don’t always flush into the deep ocean—-lots of this material is settling and accumulating on the upper slope, even over multiple seismic events. By recognizing the fingerprints of floods and earthquakes, this research improves how we interpret ancient sediment records, and shows that canyon channels act as a complex filter on downslope flow.
