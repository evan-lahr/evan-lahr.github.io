Submarine canyons are like underwater archives, recording events that shape our planet’s margins. In Astoria Canyon, recent major floods and powerful earthquakes have left distinct sediment layers that reveal how these forces move material along the seafloor. By analyzing sediment cores for age, composition, and density, we differentiate between flood-deposited layers rich in river sediments and earthquake-triggered debris flows carrying marine material. Specifically, we date layers to the 1948 and 1894 floods, and a major debris flow layer to the 1700 earthquake. What makes Astoria Canyon a special place to study this is its ~300 record preserved in oxygen-poor waters, preventing animals from burrowing through--and destroying--these layers. 

Flood turbidites are typically thin and laminated with terrestrial material, while earthquake-driven deposits are meter-scale and far more energetic, carrying individual clasts up to 50 cm in diameter. These contrasts allow scientists to separate the origins and triggers of sediment layers which otherwise may be conflated. Understanding these differences helps clarify which events actually triggered the sediment movement and how they shape the canyon over time.

The study shows that canyon heads continue to initiate sediment gravity flows during sea level highstand. Also, massive earthquake-triggered deposits don’t always flush straight to the deep ocean—-even major events can settle and accumulate on the upper slope over multiple events. By recognizing the fingerprints of floods and earthquakes, this research improves how we interpret ancient sediment records, and shows that canyon channels act as complex filters on downslope flows. This study documents the possibility of both false positives and false negatives in the abyssal turbidite record.
